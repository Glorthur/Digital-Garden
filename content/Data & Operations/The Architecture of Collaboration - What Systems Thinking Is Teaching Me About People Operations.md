The part of _Thinking in Systems_ that first stayed with me was the bathtub.

The image is almost too plain: water in the tub is the stock, water coming in is the inflow, water draining out is the outflow. I thought I understood it, then found myself thinking about it later while looking at work. Trust started to look like a stock. So did team energy, institutional knowledge, follow-through, patience, even the quiet willingness people have to speak honestly in a meeting. These things do not disappear all at once. They leak. They get used up. And by the time everyone can see the level is low, the damage has usually been happening for a while.

A familiar operating pattern made it click for me: a meeting ends with three follow-ups, two decisions still half-made, and one person quietly becoming the human glue for work that should have been clearer in the first place. I am raising my hand here because I know that role. On paper, the issue looks small. Send the recap. Chase the owner. Update the tracker. But if the same pattern keeps repeating, the follow-up list becomes evidence.

That changed how I was thinking about People Operations. I used to think the work was mostly about making people processes cleaner: better hiring rhythms, better onboarding, better meeting notes, better follow-through. I still care about those things. I probably care about them more than most people would find reasonable. But I am starting to see them as part of something larger: the conditions that make some behaviors easy and others expensive.

I work around the messy middle of projects, people, and operations: meetings, hiring rhythms, onboarding, dashboards, process cleanup, decision tracking, the unglamorous work of keeping execution moving without letting quality collapse. Because of that, I spend a lot of time close to the places where work either flows or gets stuck. A missed handoff can turn into three extra meetings. An unclear decision can keep showing up as "lack of ownership." A hiring delay can start to look like a motivation problem after the existing team has been carrying too much for too long.

Before reading Meadows, I would have described many of these as coordination problems. Sometimes I would have thought of them as management problems, communication problems, or people not being proactive enough. I still think those things can be true. But I am becoming less satisfied with stopping there.

==The question I keep coming back to now is: what is the system making normal?==

By "People Operations," I mean the operating conditions around people: how decisions get made, how work is staffed, how information moves, how managers are supported, how hiring and onboarding work, how feedback travels, how performance is interpreted, and how the organization teaches people what behavior is safe or costly.

That is what _Thinking in Systems_ is making me practice. Less "Who is the problem?" More "What structure keeps producing this behavior?"

That question matters to me because a lot of operational work sits close to the edge between help and dependency. A good process can create clarity. It can also become a crutch. A strong follow-up habit can keep work moving, but it can also hide the fact that ownership is weak. A dashboard can make work visible, and it can make the wrong thing visible. I am learning, slowly, that the tools I use to create order can also teach the system what to rely on.

## I Used to Overvalue the Visible Problem

The visible problem is tempting because it gives you something to grab. I can feel myself reaching for it, especially when work is moving fast and everyone wants an answer.

A team is missing deadlines. A manager is not giving feedback. A new hire is struggling. A process is not being followed. A meeting keeps ending without decisions. Someone resigns and everyone suddenly wants to talk about retention.

Those are real issues. They deserve attention. I just do not trust myself as much anymore when I stop at the visible issue.

Take a meeting that ends with no clear owner for the next step. The easy interpretation is that people were careless. The more systemic interpretation asks different questions. Was the meeting designed for decision-making or just updates? Was the right person in the room? Did anyone have authority to decide? Was the context shared early enough? Were tradeoffs explicit? Is there a habit of avoiding commitment because previous decisions have been reversed by someone outside the room?

The same event looks different when I stop treating it, too quickly, as a personality failure. I still do that sometimes. I just catch it faster now.

This is where Russell Ackoff's idea helps me. A system is not the sum of its parts; it is the product of their interactions. That sentence is easy to nod along to, but it becomes more uncomfortable when I apply it to work. You can have smart people, good tools, polished templates, and still have a bad operating system. The parts can be good while the interactions between them are weak.

That is why I am becoming suspicious of fixes that only improve one part. A better meeting template may help, while the real decision problem remains untouched if nobody knows who has authority. A stronger hiring scorecard is useful, while onboarding still breaks if the team has no time to teach. Manager training has its place, while feedback culture still depends on whether managers have the time, safety, and incentives to do the work properly.

This is the first shift for me: I used to look for the broken part faster than I looked at the relationship between the parts. I am trying to reverse that order.

## Burnout Looks Different Through Stocks and Flows

The bathtub made burnout feel a little less mysterious to me, and also a little more uncomfortable.

I used to think about burnout mostly as overload: too much work, too little time. That is still true. It is just not the whole picture. Burnout also looks like a stock-and-flow problem.

A team's energy is a stock. So is trust in leadership. So is belief that effort will be matched by sane prioritization. So is the amount of useful knowledge sitting inside the team. The inflows are things like clarity, rest, help, useful feedback, realistic planning, good documentation, and decisions that actually stay decided. The outflows are context switching, rework, unclear priorities, broken promises, under-staffing, emotional labor, and meetings that create more confusion than they remove.

If the drain is open wider than the faucet, the tub empties.

That sounds basic, but organizations ignore it all the time. They add a wellness session while leaving the planning process untouched. They tell people to take time off while every deadline assumes full capacity. They hire new people only after the existing team has already been stretched for months. They ask for more transparency, then punish the first person who says the plan is unrealistic.

The lesson I am taking from Meadows is that the water level matters even before it becomes a crisis. A team does not go from healthy to burned out in one dramatic moment. The stock has been draining. People have been compensating. Standards have been quietly adjusted. Small delays have accumulated.

That is the part I recognize from operations work: the delay.

By the time a hiring need is obvious, the team may already be tired. By the time a new person starts, the team has spent weeks or months covering the gap. By the time that person is onboarded enough to help, the people who were supposed to train them may have even less patience and capacity than before. I have seen how easily that lag can make good people look less capable than they are. Then the organization looks at the situation and calls it an onboarding problem.

Maybe it is. But sometimes it is a delay problem wearing an onboarding costume.

If I misdiagnose the problem, I will design the wrong intervention. I will make the checklist better when the real issue is that the team has no protected capacity to teach. I will ask managers to be more supportive when the workload model gives them no room to manage. I will call for better communication when the actual issue is that decisions are being made too late.

Systems thinking is making me more careful, maybe more suspicious, about how quickly I name the problem.

## The Real System Is What People Learn to Expect

One sentence I keep hearing in my head is: people are not confused for long about what actually gets rewarded.

You can say collaboration matters, but if the promotion system rewards individual heroics, people will optimize for visibility. You can say quality matters, but if every timeline is treated as immovable, people will learn where to cut corners. You can say feedback matters, but if honest feedback disappears into silence, people will stop spending emotional energy on it.

That is learned behavior.

This is where feedback loops became useful for me. A reinforcing loop can build something healthy. Trust can lead to more candor. Candor can surface problems earlier. Earlier problem-solving can improve reliability. Reliability can build more trust.

But the same structure can turn destructive. Stress leads to mistakes. Mistakes lead to tighter control. Tighter control slows decisions and lowers ownership. Lower ownership creates more mistakes. Then leaders point to the mistakes as proof that tighter control was necessary.

I have seen versions of that loop in small ways: follow-ups multiply because decisions were unclear; then people become more dependent on someone else to track the work; then ownership weakens because the tracking system has become the real source of accountability. The fix helps in the short term. Over time, it can train the team to wait for the follow-up instead of owning the next move.

I live by follow-ups, so this is partly a critique of my own habits. I can feel myself wanting to rescue the thread because it is faster than letting the gap become visible. The sharper system question is: is this follow-up creating clarity, or is it compensating for a missing decision habit? Is it building accountability, or replacing it?

That is the kind of question I was not asking enough before.

The uncomfortable part is that I can see myself inside the loop. If I am too good at catching every dropped thread, I may prevent the immediate failure while also hiding the weakness that needs to be addressed. The work gets saved. The system learns that someone else will save it. That is a useful service in the moment and a dangerous pattern if it becomes permanent.

## Resistance Is Information

Peter Senge's line that "the harder you push, the harder the system pushes back" makes more sense to me now.

I used to read resistance as attitude first. Someone does not adopt the process. Someone keeps bypassing the workflow. Someone agrees in the meeting and then does the old thing anyway. The easy story is that they are stubborn, careless, or not bought in. I still feel that story forming in my head sometimes.

Sometimes people are being difficult. Some resistance is avoidance, ego, or plain bad behavior. Systems thinking still makes me ask what the resistance is protecting.

Maybe the new process adds visibility but no value for the person doing the work. Maybe the old workaround exists because the official path is too slow. Maybe people do not trust that the new policy will last, because they have watched too many changes get announced and abandoned. Maybe the person resisting has learned, from experience, that formal authority says one thing while informal power rewards another.

Resistance can be a signal that the design is incomplete.

This is especially important in People Operations because people-facing work can become cosmetic if we are not careful. We can rename values, rewrite policies, refresh templates, and launch training while the deeper incentives remain untouched. The language changes, but the old system keeps its job.

I am trying to become less defensive when a process does not land. My instinct is usually to explain the process better. I am learning to pause there. I want my first questions to be more practical: does the process reduce friction or add it, does it match how decisions actually get made, who pays the cost of maintaining it, what informal workaround is it competing with, and what fear or incentive keeps the old behavior alive?

Those questions make implementation more honest.

## The Knowledge Problem Is Bigger Than Documentation

Another thing I keep noticing is how often collaboration breaks because knowledge does not move well.

I used to think of documentation as the answer to that. Write the process down. Capture the decision. Put it in the right workspace. Build the tracker. Make the dashboard. Those things matter. I still believe in them.

But documentation is only one part of knowledge flow.

A team can have documents everywhere and still not know what is going on. The issue may be that the documents are stale, or nobody trusts them, or the real context lives in private messages, or decisions are captured without the reasoning behind them. Knowledge can exist and still be trapped in the wrong place, available too late, or accessible only to people with the right relationships.

This has changed how I think about onboarding. A new person needs more than a checklist. They need a map of how the system thinks. Who decides what. Which meetings matter. Where context lives. What tradeoffs keep repeating. What the team says it values versus what it actually protects under pressure.

That kind of knowledge cannot be dumped into a handbook once and forgotten. It has to flow through meetings, notes, rituals, review cycles, and everyday decisions.

So when I think about People Operations now, I think less about "Do we have a process?" and more about "Can the right knowledge reach the right person early enough to change the outcome?"

That is a harder standard. It is also more useful.

## I Am Trying Not to Make Systems Thinking a Personality

There is a trap here, and I can feel myself walking toward it sometimes.

Once you start seeing systems, you can become annoying. Everything becomes a loop. Every problem becomes structural. Every simple fix looks shallow. You can start sounding wise while doing very little. "It is all connected" can become a very polished way to avoid choosing.

I do not want that.

Some problems need direct action. If someone is causing harm, the answer is not to hide behind a diagram. If a process is obviously broken, fix it. If a deadline is unrealistic, say so plainly. If a manager is failing people, do not turn that into a vague meditation on complexity.

Systems thinking should not make me less accountable. If it is useful at all, it should make me more precise.

It should help me ask better questions before I act, not delay action forever. It should make me more willing to test small changes and watch for feedback. It should make me more aware that my own tools, metrics, and meeting rhythms are also interventions in the system.

 I am not outside the system. If I am being honest, that is the part I would rather skip.

The dashboard I build changes what people pay attention to. The meeting notes I write shape what becomes official. The follow-up I chase changes what people treat as urgent. The onboarding flow I design shapes what a new person believes the team values. Even the questions I ask in a meeting can make some things speakable and other things harder to name.

That is a lot of responsibility for work that can look, from the outside, like admin.

But maybe that is the point. The "small" operating details are not small if they shape what the system repeats. This is where the book keeps pulling me back to myself.

## What I Am Carrying Forward

I am still early in this. I do not want to pretend that reading one book has made me a systems thinker. Mostly, it has made me slower to accept the first explanation, which is probably a decent start.

When I see a recurring problem now, I want to ask what is accumulating and what is draining. I want to look for the delay between action and consequence. I want to notice when a quick fix is quietly becoming a dependency. I want to pay attention to what people have learned to expect from the policy, the incentives, and the way decisions actually get made.

Most of all, I want to stop treating collaboration as a personality trait.

Collaboration has an architecture. It is built out of decision rights, information flow, trust, capacity, incentives, timing, and the small rituals that teach people how work really happens. When that architecture is weak, asking people to "collaborate better" starts to sound like asking water to stay in a tub while the drain is still open.

The question I am left with is not, "How do I fix the people?"

It is: "What am I helping the system make normal?"

That question is uncomfortable because it brings me into the frame. It asks me to look at the processes I maintain, the meetings I structure, the follow-ups I chase, the assumptions I carry, and the signals I help amplify. It asks me to be less impressed by clean fixes and more attentive to the conditions those fixes enter.

That is what _Thinking in Systems_ is teaching me right now. I do not need to become the person who sees every loop. I want to become the kind of operator who notices what the loop is doing to people before the tub is empty.

## Notes

This essay draws primarily from Donella Meadows' _Thinking in Systems_, especially her explanations of stocks, flows, feedback loops, delays, bounded rationality, and the humility required to work with complex systems.

It also lightly draws on Russell Ackoff's argument that systems are defined by interactions between parts, and Peter Senge's writing on feedback, resistance, and the discipline of seeing our own role inside the systems we want to change.
